What this concrete calculator does

Concrete is ordered and priced in two completely different ways depending on how much you need. Small jobs use bagged pre-mixed concrete, sold by weight in 40, 60 and 80 pound sacks at a hardware store. Anything from roughly one cubic yard upward usually comes from a ready-mix truck and is billed by the cubic yard, with a minimum order and a short delivery window. This calculator handles both: it reports the volume in the units a ready-mix plant quotes, then translates that volume into whole bags of each common sack size, so you can compare the two routes before you leave the house.

It is deliberately a rectangular slab calculator. Patios, shed floors, garage aprons, hot-tub pads, sidewalk sections and post footings formed as square boxes are all rectangular prisms, and that covers most DIY pours. If your project is a circle, a ring, or a shape made of several rectangles, pour it in sections or add the pieces together.

The three measurements that matter

Length and width define the area. Measure the inside face of the forms, not the outside, because the form boards themselves are not part of the slab. Measure twice: a 2 inch error across a 12 foot side changes the volume by about 1.4 percent, which is roughly one extra 80 lb bag per 100 square feet.

Thickness is where most estimates go wrong. A slab specified as “four inches” is often formed with 2×4 lumber, which is actually 3½ inches deep. That half inch is about a 12 percent difference in volume. Use the thickness you intend to pour, and if you are forming with 2×4s on a compacted base, enter 3.5 inches rather than 4 unless you plan to grade the subbase to make up the difference.

Waste allowance is the fudge factor for spillage, uneven subgrade, over-excavated spots and the inch or two left in the wheelbarrow. It is not optional in practice; it is optional in the calculator only so you can see the difference.

The formulas this page uses

Every number on the right side of the page comes from these six lines. There is nothing else happening behind the scenes.

volume (ft³) = length (ft) × width (ft) × thickness (ft) thickness (ft) = thickness (in) ÷ 12 or thickness (cm) ÷ 30.48 volume (yd³) = volume (ft³) ÷ 27 volume (m³) = volume (ft³) × 0.028316846592 volume + waste = volume (ft³) × (1 + waste % ÷ 100) bags = ⌈ (volume + waste) ÷ bag yield ⌉

The bag yield figures are the standard published numbers for pre-mixed concrete: an 80 lb bag makes 0.60 cubic feet, a 60 lb bag makes 0.45 cubic feet, and a 40 lb bag makes 0.30 cubic feet. Those yields assume the bag is mixed exactly as directed and that you do not lose material to a sloppy mix. The calculator rounds the bag count up to a whole bag, because a store will not sell you a partial sack and running short mid-pour is far worse than having one bag left over.

Unit conversions run in both directions. Feet convert to metres at 3.28084 ft per metre, and cubic feet convert to cubic metres at 0.0283168 m³ per ft³, which is the same as dividing by 35.3147. Entering 4 m by 3 m by 10 cm and entering 13 ft 1 in by 9 ft 10 in by 4 in describe almost exactly the same slab, and the totals agree to within a rounding error.

Worked example: a 10 × 10 ft patio, 4 inches thick

The classic patio pour

Area = 10 × 10 = 100 ft². Thickness = 4 ÷ 12 = 0.3333 ft. Volume = 100 × 0.3333 = 33.33 ft³. In cubic yards that is 33.33 ÷ 27 = 1.235 yd³. Adding a 10 percent waste allowance gives 36.67 ft³.

With 80 lb bags at 0.60 ft³ each: 36.67 ÷ 0.60 = 61.11, so buy 62 bags, about 4,960 lb of material. At $5.50 a bag that is $341. The same pour in 60 lb bags needs 82 bags (0.45 ft³ each), and in 40 lb bags it needs 123. Notice that the total weight barely moves between sizes — you are paying for the same amount of powder and aggregate, just in more sacks.

Worked example: a metric shed base, 4 m × 3 m × 100 mm

Working in metres and millimetres

4 m = 13.123 ft, 3 m = 9.843 ft, and 100 mm = 10 cm = 0.3281 ft. Volume = 13.123 × 9.843 × 0.3281 = 42.38 ft³, which is 1.200 m³ or 1.570 yd³. With a 5 percent allowance the order becomes 44.50 ft³.

In 60 lb bags that is 44.50 ÷ 0.45 = 98.9, so 99 bags — around 5,940 lb. If you were ordering ready-mix instead, you would ask for 1.25 m³ and be done in one delivery. That is the point where bagged concrete stops making sense: 99 sacks is a long day, and the truck is usually cheaper per cubic metre once you pass about one cubic metre.

Worked example: a 12 ft 6 in × 8 ft pad, 6 inches thick

Mixed units and a thicker slab

12 ft 6 in = 12.5 ft, width 8 ft, thickness 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 ft. Volume = 12.5 × 8 × 0.5 = 50 ft³ = 1.852 yd³. With no waste allowance, 40 lb bags give 50 ÷ 0.30 = 166.7, so 167 bags.

This pad holds a vehicle or a heavy hot tub, so it is 6 inches thick rather than 4. Thickness drives volume linearly: going from 4 to 6 inches adds 50 percent to the concrete, the bags and the cost. A 4 inch version of the same pad would need only 112 bags of 40 lb mix. Thicken only where the load or the soil requires it, and put the money into a proper compacted base instead.

Choosing a waste allowance

  • 0–5 percent: flat, formed slabs on a laser-leveled base, poured by someone who has done it before. Even professionals rarely use zero.
  • 10 percent: the safe default for patios, shed floors and walkways on a normal compacted base.
  • 15 percent: uneven or over-excavated subgrade, deep or irregular forms, a first pour, or a job where the truck has a long wheelbarrow run and some material will be lost to the ground.
  • 20 percent or more: footings dug by hand into irregular holes, or small repairs where you mix in a bucket and leave residue behind.

Waste is not the same as over-ordering for ready-mix. Ready-mix plants typically charge for what they send, and many have a minimum of one cubic yard plus a short-load fee below three or four yards. Ordering 0.25 cubic yards extra is cheap insurance; ordering a full yard extra is not.

Bags or a ready-mix truck?

The crossover is surprisingly low. An 80 lb bag yields 0.60 ft³, so a cubic yard (27 ft³) takes 45 bags and weighs about 3,600 lb — more than one and a half tonnes of sacks to move, open and mix. Most people find that around 60 to 80 bags is the practical ceiling for a single weekend pour with a rented mixer and one helper, which is a little over one and a half cubic yards.

Below that, bags win on flexibility: you can buy them in stages, return unopened sacks, and pour in sections without a deadline. Above it, ready-mix wins on labour. A truck with a chute places a cubic yard in minutes, and many suppliers will let you order in 0.25 yard increments. If the truck cannot reach the pour, a trailer-mounted mixer or a pump adds cost that can tip the decision back toward bags.

Frequently asked questions

How many 80 lb bags of concrete make a cubic yard?

About 45. A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet and an 80 lb bag yields roughly 0.60 cubic feet, so 27 ÷ 0.60 = 45 bags. In 60 lb bags it takes 60 (27 ÷ 0.45), and in 40 lb bags 90 (27 ÷ 0.30).

How much does a cubic yard of concrete weigh?

Around 4,050 lb (about 1,840 kg), or roughly 2,400 kg per cubic metre. Mix design changes the number a little: a heavier aggregate mix can reach 4,200 lb per yard, while a lightweight mix is considerably less. For planning, 150 lb per cubic foot is the usual rule of thumb.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

Four inches is the standard for patios, walkways, shed floors and hot-tub pads on firm, well-drained, compacted ground. Use 5 inches for a driveway carrying cars and 6 inches for trucks, RVs or heavy equipment — and add a compacted gravel base of 4 to 6 inches underneath in either case. Thickness cannot compensate for a bad base.

Does the calculator include the gravel base?

No. It estimates concrete only. A typical base is 4 inches of compacted crushed stone, which for a 100 ft² patio is about 1.25 cubic yards loose. Order that separately, and remember it compacts by roughly 20 percent.

Why did my bag count come out higher than the store’s chart?

Usually because of the waste allowance. A store chart typically quotes the theoretical count for an exact volume, often at 4 inches thick. Add 10 percent for waste and round up to whole bags and the number rises. If the gap is large, check whether the chart assumes a different bag yield — some brands print 0.55 ft³ for an 80 lb bag rather than 0.60.

Can I use this for a circular slab or footings?

For a circle, measure the diameter, then use area = π × (diameter ÷ 2)² and multiply by the thickness. For a post hole, treat the hole as a cylinder: volume = π × radius² × depth. Then compare that volume against the bag yields above. The bag table here is still useful once you know the volume in cubic feet.

How accurate is the cost estimate?

The arithmetic is exact for the price per bag you enter. Real checkout totals vary with sales tax, delivery, pallet deposits and whether the store discounts by the pallet — 42 or 56 bags depending on the brand. Use the cost figure as a budget number and confirm it at the store.

Before you order

Write down the length, width and thickness in the same units you measured, then add the waste allowance once, at the end. Check that your form depth matches the thickness you entered, and remember that a slab poured against a house needs an expansion joint and a slight slope away from the building — neither of which changes the volume much but both of which change how the job goes. Finally, if the count is over about 80 bags, price a ready-mix delivery as well; the comparison often surprises people.
