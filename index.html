Twelve focused tools · no sign-up

Numbers you can build, price, drive, stream and play on.

Toolbench is a small collection of free calculators for people who would rather not guess: how much concrete a slab needs, how many boards a deck takes, how many risers a flight of stairs needs, what a marketplace or a streaming platform actually pays you, which health plan is cheaper after a job ends, how long a battery will run, what a 3D print really costs, and what your photos reveal about where you took them. Each tool runs entirely in your browser, loads in well under a second, and never asks for an account.

Built to be boring in the best way

A calculator should be quick, correct and repeatable. That is the whole product brief.

Every formula is written down

Each tool page explains its inputs, shows the exact equations it uses, and walks through three worked examples with numbers you can check by hand. No hidden magic, no vague "estimated" numbers.

Your inputs stay on your device

All arithmetic happens in your browser with plain JavaScript. There is no account, no upload, and no calculator history on a server. The EXIF stripper goes further: your photos are decoded and re-encoded locally, never sent anywhere.

Assumptions are labelled

Marketplace fee rates, material densities, insurance premiums, battery chemistry limits and game sensitivity values change over time. Where a default is used, the page says so, shows the figure, and lets you override it with your own numbers.

Who these calculators are for

Homeowners and small contractors pouring a shed base, a patio, a driveway apron or a set of fence-post footings. Ready-mix is sold by the cubic yard in the US and by the cubic metre almost everywhere else, while bagged concrete is sold by weight — and the two never line up neatly. The concrete estimator converts between them, adds the waste allowance that a real pour needs, and rounds up to whole bags, because you cannot buy 61.1 bags.

Landscapers and weekend gardeners ordering gravel, mulch, topsoil or a driveway base. The landscape yard quotes cubic yards, the quarry may price by the ton, and the shop sells two-cubic-foot bags, all for the same pile. The gravel and mulch estimator converts between all three, weights each material properly (a yard of mulch is 0.40 ton, a yard of crushed stone is 1.40), and then switches to deck takeoff when the project is timber rather than stone.

Anyone building a stair from a deck to a garden, or a basement to a first floor. The rise is the only measurement you have and the rest has to be derived: how many risers that rise divides into, how deep the treads can be, how long the stringer needs to be, and whether the result is actually legal. The stair calculator does that division and then checks the four IRC R311.7 limits that a residential stair most often breaks. The same page answers the roof question that follows a new deck: what a pitch means in degrees, and how long the rafters need to be.

Online sellers on Etsy and TikTok Shop who price a product off the material cost and then discover that fees, payment processing and ads ate the margin. The seller calculator shows gross revenue, every fee line, your net profit per order, and the item price at which the order stops losing money.

Owner-operators and delivery drivers deciding whether a load is worth taking. A $2.40 rate looks fine until the deadhead, the broker’s cut, the factoring fee and the maintenance reserve are counted. The trucking calculator turns a rate confirmation into cost per mile, net profit, profit per hour and a break-even rate you can quote from.

Streamers and creators looking at a payout that does not match the audience. Bits, subscriptions and Super Chats each pay differently, the app stores take their 30 percent before the platform splits anything, and withholding depends on paperwork filed years ago. The payout calculator shows the waterfall for Twitch, YouTube and Kick, and what the same month would pay on each.

Anyone leaving a job and facing the COBRA envelope versus the marketplace. The comparison depends on premiums, what part of the deductible is already met, how much care you expect and where the out-of-pocket cap sits. The health insurance calculator adds those up over the exact number of months you need cover, and shows where the answer flips.

Drivers thinking about an electric car, and EV owners checking that the saving is real. Electricity and petrol are priced in different units, and public fast charging can erase the advantage that home charging creates. The EV calculator splits the two and reports cost per 100 miles, per year and over five years.

People sizing a battery for a router that must survive an outage, a CPAP, a camper or a shed. A 100Ah label promises more than it delivers once the chemistry’s usable depth, the inverter’s losses and Peukert are counted. The battery calculator applies all three and shows the same pack in lithium and lead side by side.

3D printing sellers who quote filament and nothing else. Purge for multi-colour prints, supports, electricity, printer wear, failures and marketplace fees all live in the gap between the filament cost and the price. The print cost calculator fills that gap in and returns a price that holds a margin.

Anyone about to post a photo of something they are selling, of their children, or of their home. The file carries the camera, the timestamp and often the GPS coordinates, and it looks identical in every preview. The metadata stripper shows what is in there and rewrites the file from pixels only, inside the browser.

FPS players who switch between games and want the same feel on the mouse. Sensitivity numbers are not portable between titles: a Valorant sensitivity of 0.35 means something completely different from 0.35 in CS2. The converter works from the physical measurement that does transfer — the distance your mouse travels for a full 360° turn — and reports it in centimetres and inches along with eDPI.

How the site stays fast

Toolbench is a static site: hand-written HTML, one stylesheet, and a few kilobytes of JavaScript. There are no web fonts, no analytics scripts, no frameworks and no third-party embeds. Pages are served from GitHub Pages, so the first load is a handful of small files and every visit after that comes from your browser cache. The calculators are unit-tested with the same JavaScript that runs in the page, which is why a wrong number is a bug you can find rather than a guess you have to trust.
