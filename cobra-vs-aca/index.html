The choice after a job ends

When group health coverage stops, most people in the United States have two realistic ways to keep insured: elect COBRA continuation coverage through the old employer’s plan, or buy a plan on the ACA marketplace. Both are real insurance with real networks and real deductibles, and both cost money every month. The comparison that matters is not the sticker price of the premium but the total you would pay over the exact months you need cover — premium plus whatever the plan makes you pay when you actually use it.

That total depends on three things that are easy to miss. First, how much of the annual deductible and out-of-pocket maximum you have already satisfied this year: staying in the same plan keeps that credit, while a new plan starts from zero. Second, how much care you expect in the coming months, which can swing the answer by thousands of dollars. Third, whether a premium tax credit applies to the marketplace plan, which can make it dramatically cheaper than the full price you see before entering income details.

This calculator models those three things and shows where the two options cross over. It is deliberately simple: premiums, the remaining deductible, coinsurance on the rest, and a cap at the remaining out-of-pocket maximum. That covers the large majority of real decisions, and it makes every assumption visible so you can adjust it.

What COBRA actually is

COBRA is a federal law that lets you keep the group plan you had at work after you leave, at your own expense. Your employer stops paying its share, so you pay the full premium plus an administration charge of up to 2 percent, which is why the monthly figure is often two to three times what was deducted from your paycheque. The plan itself does not change: same network, same deductible, same prescriptions, same claims history, and the same out-of-pocket spending you have already accumulated this year.

You generally have 60 days from the notice to elect, and coverage is retroactive to the day the group coverage ended. That retroactivity is a genuine advantage: if something happens in the first weeks, you can elect afterwards and still have the claim covered. It is also a trap, because the premiums for those retroactive months come due at once, and people who assumed COBRA was free until they signed something are surprised by a bill.

Duration depends on the qualifying event. A voluntary quit or a reduction in hours typically gives 18 months; divorce, a dependent ageing out or a death in the family can give 36. The temporary federal subsidy that made COBRA free for a period during 2021 and 2022 has ended, so unless your former employer is running its own subsidy, the full premium is yours.

What the ACA marketplace offers instead

A marketplace plan is individual insurance bought through the health insurance exchange. Plans are sorted into metal tiers — bronze, silver, gold and platinum — which describe how the cost is split between you and the insurer rather than the quality of care. Bronze has the lowest premium and the highest deductible; silver sits in the middle and is the only tier that can carry extra cost-sharing reductions if your income qualifies; gold costs more per month and pays more of each bill.

The price you actually pay depends on household income, because premium tax credits are calculated against the federal poverty level. Two neighbours can be quoted very different premiums for the same plan. That is why the input on this page says “after credit”: enter the number the marketplace showed once your income and household details were in, not the pre-credit list price.

Timing matters too. Losing job-based coverage opens a special enrollment period — generally 60 days before and after the coverage ends — during which you can buy without waiting for open enrollment. Outside that window the annual open enrollment period is November 1 to January 15 in most states, though some run their own exchange with different dates. Networks are narrower than most group plans, so check that your doctors and hospital are in the plan before comparing prices.

The formula behind the comparison

For each plan, over the number of months you enter:

premium cost = monthly premium × months deductible left = max(0, deductible − already met) deductible paid = min(expected care, deductible left) coinsurance = (expected care − deductible paid) × (your share % ÷ 100) care cost before cap= deductible paid + coinsurance out of pocket = min(care cost before cap, out-of-pocket max − already paid) total cost = premium cost + out of pocket

The order matters. Care spending fills the remaining deductible first, at 100 percent your cost, and only the amount above the deductible attracts coinsurance. The out-of-pocket maximum then caps the total for covered, in-network care, but not the premium — a distinction that catches people out when they budget for a bad year. Prescription copays, which this model folds into “expected care” as spending, are usually included in the out-of-pocket maximum; premiums never are.

The model assumes the expected care figure is the allowed amount after any network discounts, which is how insurers report it. If you enter the billed charge instead, the deductible and coinsurance will look worse than reality. Use the number on an explanation of benefits for similar care, or a rough figure from your own history.

Worked example: six months, typical use

The middle of the road

COBRA costs $720 a month, so six months of premium is $4,320. The deductible is $3,000 and $1,200 is already met, leaving $1,800. With $3,500 of expected care, the first $1,800 fills the deductible and the remaining $1,700 attracts 20 percent coinsurance, or $340. Care costs $2,140, under the remaining out-of-pocket cap of $7,000, so the COBRA total is $6,460.

The marketplace plan is $480 a month, or $2,880 for six months, and its $4,500 deductible is untouched. The full $3,500 of care goes to the deductible, so the total is $6,380 — $80 less than COBRA. That is a genuinely close call, and it is the most common result: the marketplace premium advantage roughly cancels the COBRA deductible already met.

Worked example: a bad year

High use, $15,000 of care

COBRA: premium $4,320; the $1,800 deductible is paid, then 20 percent of $13,200 is $2,640, for $4,440 of care. The remaining out-of-pocket cap is $7,000, so the care cost stands. Total $8,760.

Marketplace: premium $2,880; $4,500 deductible, then 20 percent of $10,500 is $2,100, for $6,600 of care, under its $9,200 cap. Total $9,480. Now COBRA wins by $720, because the deductible already met is worth more than the premium saving. Push the care higher and both plans approach their out-of-pocket maximums, at which point the comparison is simply premium plus remaining cap — and COBRA’s already-met spending usually keeps it ahead.

Worked example: healthy and lucky

Low use, $600 of care

COBRA: $4,320 of premium plus $600 of care under the remaining deductible = $4,920. Marketplace: $2,880 plus $600 = $3,480. The marketplace saves $1,440, and the gap widens further if a tax credit is involved. For someone young, healthy and not mid-treatment, the premium difference dominates and COBRA rarely makes sense unless the group plan is unusually good or a specific specialist only takes that network.

What the calculator deliberately leaves out

  • Dental and vision. COBRA can often be elected separately for these; a marketplace plan may include paediatric dental only. Add those premiums by hand if they matter.
  • Prescription formularies. A $400-a-month drug can make a cheaper premium irrelevant. Check the formulary and tier of each medication before deciding.
  • Networks and travel. A marketplace HMO may not cover the specialist you have been seeing for years, and out-of-network care does not count toward the in-network out-of-pocket cap in the way this model assumes.
  • HSA contributions. A high-deductible marketplace plan may let you open an HSA, which is worth real money in tax terms. COBRA plans generally are not HSA-eligible.
  • Future income changes. Premium tax credits are reconciled on the tax return. If your income rises after you enrol, part of the credit may have to be repaid.

Frequently asked questions

Is COBRA always more expensive than a marketplace plan?

No. COBRA usually has a higher monthly premium, but it keeps the deductible and out-of-pocket spending you have already accumulated, and it keeps your network and prescriptions exactly as they were. In a year with significant medical costs, especially after a deductible is partly met, COBRA can be the cheaper option despite the higher premium.

Can I switch from COBRA to a marketplace plan later?

Usually yes, but the timing rules are strict. Dropping COBRA voluntarily generally does not open a special enrollment period, so you may have to wait for open enrollment unless you have another qualifying event. Check with the marketplace before cancelling anything, and do not create a gap in coverage while you decide.

What counts as “expected care” in this calculator?

The total allowed charges for medical care you expect during the months you need cover — office visits, labs, imaging, procedures, prescriptions — before insurance pays its share but after network discounts. If you have no idea, use your last year’s total from your explanation of benefits, scaled to the number of months.

Why does the calculator cap care costs at the out-of-pocket maximum?

Because that is what the plan does. Once you have paid the annual out-of-pocket maximum for covered, in-network care, the insurer pays 100 percent of further covered care for the rest of the plan year. The cap is reduced here by whatever you have already paid this year, so the model does not overstate a plan you have been using.

Does a marketplace premium tax credit count as income?

No, the credit is not taxable income. It is applied to your premium in advance based on estimated income, then reconciled when you file. If your actual income comes in higher than estimated, you may owe some of it back, so update your income estimate with the marketplace when it changes.

What happens if I do nothing?

COBRA is not automatic. If you do not elect within the window, the coverage ends and you have no insurance through that plan. The federal penalty for being uninsured is zero, but the financial risk is not: a single hospital stay can exceed a year of premiums. If you are unsure, elect COBRA to preserve the retroactive option and cancel later if you find something better — read the plan’s cancellation terms first.

How to use the answer

Start with the scenario you think is most likely, then read the table below it. If one plan wins in every column, the decision is easy. If the winner flips between low and high use — as it often does — the question becomes how much risk you can absorb. Choosing the cheaper plan and banking the difference works when you are healthy and the difference is large; choosing the plan with the deductible already met works when you know care is coming. Either way, confirm the network and the drug formulary before you pay the first premium, because a plan that does not cover your doctor is expensive at any price.
