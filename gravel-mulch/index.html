Two material problems that look alike

Buying gravel, mulch or topsoil is a volume problem dressed up as a weight problem. The landscape yard sells by the cubic yard, the bagged product in the aisle is sold by volume in cubic feet, and the quarry ticket may be priced by the ton. All three describe the same pile, and the conversion between them depends on what the material weighs — which is why a cubic yard of mulch is less than half the weight of a cubic yard of crushed stone, and why the same money buys very different amounts.

Decking is the opposite kind of problem: a linear takeoff. You know the deck’s length and width, and you need to know how many boards to buy, how many joists they land on and roughly how many screws disappear into the job. It is geometry rather than density, but the same rule applies — measure the actual opening, add a sensible waste allowance, and round up to what the store actually sells.

Both live on this page because both usually come up in the same weekend project: a deck, a gravel path around it, and fresh mulch in the beds beside it. If you are pouring concrete instead, the concrete slab and bag estimator handles the slab and the base underneath it.

Volume first, then weight

Everything starts with the area of the bed or the driveway, multiplied by the depth you want. Length times width gives square feet; square feet times depth in feet gives cubic feet. One cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so dividing by 27 converts to the unit the landscape yard quotes. If you measure in metres, one cubic metre is 35.31 cubic feet, and the page converts both ways so you can order in whichever unit the supplier prefers.

area (ft²) = length (ft) × width (ft) depth (ft) = depth (in) ÷ 12 or depth (cm) ÷ 30.48 volume (ft³) = area × depth (ft) volume (yd³) = volume (ft³) ÷ 27 volume (m³) = volume (ft³) ÷ 35.3147 with waste = volume × (1 + waste % ÷ 100) tons = volume (yd³) × density (ton/yd³) 2 cu ft bags = ⌈ volume (ft³) ÷ 2 ⌉

Density is the only figure here that varies by material, and it varies a lot. The calculator carries typical loose bulk densities: 1.35 tons per cubic yard for pea gravel, 1.40 for 3/4 inch crushed stone, 1.30 for river rock, 1.35 for damp sand, 1.10 for screened topsoil, 0.55 for compost and 0.40 for shredded bark mulch. Suppliers sometimes quote pounds per cubic yard instead, in which case divide by 2,000 to get short tons. If your supplier prints a number on the ticket, use theirs — the field is editable for exactly that reason.

Bagged material is quoted by volume, not weight, because the bag is filled by volume and the moisture content changes the weight from pallet to pallet. A “2 cubic foot” bag is the standard for mulch and most soils; some gravel and decorative stone come in 0.5 cubic foot bags, so check the label before trusting the bag count. This page assumes 2 cubic feet per bag, which is the common size in US garden centres.

Depths that actually work

  • Mulch in beds: 2 to 3 inches. Below 2 inches weeds push through and the soil dries out; above 4 inches the mulch can mat, shed water and hold moisture against stems. Topping up an existing bed needs only 1 to 2 inches.
  • Gravel paths and dog runs: 3 to 4 inches of stone over a weed barrier. Pea gravel moves underfoot; crushed stone locks together better on a slope.
  • Driveway base: 4 inches compacted, which means ordering 5 inches loose because crushed stone compacts by roughly 20 percent. Two lifts of 2 inches each compact better than one 4 inch lift.
  • Topsoil for a lawn: 4 to 6 inches of screened loam, and expect it to settle 10 to 15 percent after the first season. For a raised bed, 8 to 12 inches is not unusual.
  • Drainage rock around a foundation: 4 to 6 inches of washed stone, with the fabric underneath doing the actual work of keeping fines out.

Worked example: mulch for a 20 × 10 ft bed

Two inches over 200 square feet

Area is 20 × 10 = 200 ft². At 2 inches the depth is 0.1667 ft, so the volume is 33.33 ft³, or 1.235 yd³. Adding 5 percent for waste gives 35.0 ft³ and 1.296 yd³. Bark mulch at 0.40 ton per yard weighs just 0.52 ton — light enough that a half-ton pickup can carry it in one trip.

In 2 cubic foot bags that is 35 ÷ 2 = 17.5, so buy 18 bags. At $38 a cubic yard delivered, the bulk price is $49.26; at $4.50 a bag the bagged price would be $81, which is the usual premium for someone else doing the bagging. One cubic yard covers 162 ft² at this depth, a useful number to remember when you are standing in the yard guessing.

Worked example: a metric driveway base

4 m × 3 m at 5 cm of pea gravel

Four metres is 13.12 ft and three metres is 9.84 ft, so the area is 129.17 ft². Five centimetres is 0.164 ft, giving 21.19 ft³ — 0.6 m³ or 0.785 yd³. At 1.35 ton per yard that is 1.06 tons, and at $55 a ton the gravel costs $58.27.

At this size, bags are not realistic: 21.19 ft³ is 11 bags of 2 cubic feet, which is fine for a path but silly for a driveway. Bulk delivery usually has a minimum of one cubic yard or a small-load fee, so the honest comparison is bulk delivered versus bagged picked up, including the fuel and the second trip.

Worked example: deck boards for a 16 × 12 ft deck

5.5 inch boards, 1/8 inch gap, 12 ft stock

Each board plus its gap covers 5.625 inches of deck width. A 12 ft wide deck is 144 inches, so 144 ÷ 5.625 = 25.6, which rounds up to 26 rows. Each row runs the 16 ft length, so the surface needs 26 × 16 = 416 linear feet. Add 5 percent for cuts and defects: 436.8 ft. Buying 12 ft boards, 436.8 ÷ 12 = 36.4, so 37 boards, with 7.2 ft of offcut left over.

Joists at 16 inches on centre across a 16 ft span: 192 ÷ 16 = 12 spaces, plus one for the end joist, so 13 joists. Two screws at every crossing is 37 × 13 × 2 = 962 screws, and with a 5 percent spare, 1,011 screws. At $18 a board the decking is $666 before fasteners, joists, hangers and the beer you will owe your helper.

How the deck takeoff works

The row count is the part people get wrong. Boards are laid parallel to the deck’s length, so the number of rows depends on the width, not the area. Divide the width in inches by the board width plus the gap, then round up: you cannot lay 25.6 rows, and the last row is usually ripped narrower to fit against the house or the rim joist.

Waste matters more than it looks. A 5 percent allowance covers the inevitable short cut at the far end, a board with a bad crown, and the two or three that get dropped. Diagonal layouts, picture frames, stairs, and decks with more than four corners push waste to 10 or 15 percent. If you are buying 16 ft boards for a 16 ft deck, remember that the actual board may be a fraction short of nominal, so allow for that gap at the rim.

Joist count uses the same idea in the other direction: the joists run across the width at a fixed spacing, and the count is the length in inches divided by the spacing, plus one for the end. Screw counts assume two fasteners per board per joist, which is the standard for a face-screwed deck. Hidden fastener systems use a clip at every joist instead and often work out at one clip per board per joist plus a starter clip per row, so the number of fasteners is roughly the same but the price is not.

Bags, bulk or a truckload?

Bagged material wins for small jobs and for anything you have to carry through a gate or up stairs. It loses badly on price per cubic foot once you pass about 10 cubic feet: a pallet of 65 two-cubic-foot bags is only 4.8 cubic yards, and the per-yard price is usually two to three times the bulk price. Bulk delivery wins whenever the truck can reach the spot, but check the minimum order, the delivery fee and whether the driver can dump exactly where you want it — a pile in the street is a wheelbarrow problem you did not budget for.

Tonnage pricing is common at quarries and aggregate yards. It is honest, because the truck is weighed on the way in and out, but it makes comparison harder: a ton of mulch is about 2.5 cubic yards while a ton of crushed stone is about 0.71 cubic yards. The table on the results panel does that conversion for you at the volume you entered, so the same pile can be priced any of the three ways before you call.

Frequently asked questions

How many bags of mulch are in a cubic yard?

13.5 bags of 2 cubic feet, because a cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. Stores often sell mulch in 2 cubic foot bags by the pallet of 65, which is 130 cubic feet or 4.8 cubic yards. If a supplier quotes 3 cubic foot bags, a yard is 9 bags.

How much does a yard of gravel weigh?

About 1.4 short tons (2,800 lb) for 3/4 inch crushed stone, 1.35 tons for pea gravel and sand, and 1.30 tons for river rock. That is why a cubic yard of gravel is a trailer job while a cubic yard of mulch fits in a pickup bed.

Do I need a weed barrier under gravel?

For a path or a decorative bed, yes: a woven landscape fabric keeps soil from pumping up into the stone and cuts the weeding to almost nothing. Skip the thin black plastic, which tears and traps water. For a driveway base, geotextile under the stone helps separate the subgrade from the aggregate and is worth the cost on soft ground.

Can I put new mulch over old mulch?

Yes, up to a point. Rake the old layer to break the crust, pull it back from stems and the foundation, then add 1 to 2 inches. Once the total depth passes about 4 inches, remove some of the old material instead of piling more on — deep mulch holds moisture against stems and can starve roots of oxygen.

Why is my deck board count higher than the deck area suggests?

Because boards are counted in rows, not square feet. The gap, the board width and the rounding up to whole rows all add material, and so does the waste allowance. A 192 ft² deck needs 37 twelve-foot boards, which is 444 board feet — about 2.3 board feet per square foot of deck, since the gaps are not deck.

How many screws per square foot of decking?

Roughly five or six for face-screwed boards at 16 inch joist spacing, which is what the 1,011 screws on the 192 ft² example works out to. Buy a box or two extra: dropped screws, stripped heads and a driver bit that rounds off will eat the spare faster than you expect.

Before you order

Measure the actual area, not the area on the plot plan. Beds curve, driveways flare at the apron, and a deck is rarely the exact rectangle on the permit drawing. Break odd shapes into rectangles, compute each and add them up. Then decide the depth from what the material has to do, add the waste allowance once at the end, and round the order up to the unit the supplier sells — whole bags, whole yards or whole tons. One extra bag is cheap; a second delivery is not.
