Why a streamer needs a payout calculator

A channel that “makes $4.99 a sub” is a channel that has not read its own dashboard. Between the price a viewer pays and the money that arrives, three separate parties take a cut, and each of them takes it in a different order. The platform takes its share of the subscription. The app store takes its 30 percent first if the viewer subscribed on a phone. Then the tax authority takes its share of what is left, because the platform paying you is a US company. The order matters: a 30 percent fee applied before a 50/50 split costs half of what the same fee applied after would.

This page puts those three steps in the right order for each of the three big streaming platforms, shows every line, and then runs the same activity through all of them so the comparison is honest. It does not include ad revenue, tips outside the platform, sponsorships or merch, because none of those pass through the same machinery.

How each platform pays

Twitch

Bits are the simplest money on the internet: one Bit pays the streamer one cent, so 5,000 Bits is $50. Viewers buy Bits at a markup, especially in the mobile app, and that markup is Twitch's revenue, not something taken from you.

Subscriptions are a share of the net subscription price: $4.99, $9.99 and $24.99 at Tier 1, 2 and 3, split 50/50 by default. Partner Plus raises the creator share; Twitch has changed the mechanics of that program since it launched, so the checkbox here models the 70/30 version and the dashboard is the authority on your own rate. A subscription bought inside the mobile app is a different story: Apple or Google takes about 30 percent off the top before the split, which is why the same Tier 1 sub pays roughly $1.75 instead of $2.50.

YouTube

Super Chats, Super Thanks and channel memberships pay the creator 70 percent. The membership price is set per channel, so the calculator lets you enter it rather than assuming $4.99. On iOS, in-app purchases are processed by Apple, which takes its 30 percent first — the widely reported result is that a creator keeps around 49 cents of every dollar spent in the app, and the calculator reproduces that: 30 percent store fee, then 30 percent to YouTube.

Kick

Kick's selling point is a 95/5 split on subscription revenue, which is dramatically better than either competitor. The same 100 Tier 1 subscribers that pay $249.50 on Twitch pay $474.05 on Kick. Whether that advantage survives depends on audience size, not arithmetic: a platform with a tenth of the viewers is not a better deal because the percentage is higher.

Tax withholding, in one paragraph

All three platforms are US companies, and the money they pay for subscriptions, Bits and Super Chats is treated as a royalty. A creator who is not a US person is subject to US withholding at the statutory rate unless a tax treaty between their country and the United States reduces it, which requires filing the right form — a W-8BEN for an individual, a W-8BEN-E for a company. A treaty rate of zero is common, 15 percent appears in many treaties, and 30 percent applies where no benefit is claimed. This is not a platform fee and it is not lost forever: a US tax return can often recover it, which is a question for an accountant rather than a calculator. Use the rate your statement shows.

Worked example: a Twitch month with 5,000 Bits and 100 Tier 1 subs

The default numbers on this page

5,000 Bits pay $50. One hundred Tier 1 subs at $4.99 gross $499, and at 50/50 the creator's share is $249.50. The month's creator revenue is $299.50 before tax, on $549 that viewers paid — a 54.6 percent take.

Now switch the same month to Partner Plus: the sub share becomes $349.30 and the total $399.30. Switch on the mobile-app checkbox instead and the sub share falls to $174.65, a $74.85 loss on 100 subs that is easy to miss because nothing on the stream changes. Apply 30 percent withholding on the original figure and $209.65 arrives.

Worked example: YouTube Super Chats on the web versus iOS

Same generosity, two checkouts

$500 of Super Chats plus 120 members at $4.99 is $1,098.80 of gross revenue. YouTube keeps 30 percent, so the creator gets $769.16 on the web. Through the iOS app, Apple's 30 percent comes off first and YouTube takes 30 percent of what remains: $538.41. Half the money, from an audience that gave exactly the same amount.

That is why so many creators point at the web checkout in chat. The viewer pays the same either way; the difference is entirely in who else gets paid.

Worked example: the Kick comparison

100 subs and 10 Tier 2s

On Kick, 100 Tier 1 subs ($499) and 10 Tier 2 subs ($99.90) gross $598.90, and the 95 percent split pays $568.96. The same subscription mix on Twitch pays $299.45, and on YouTube as memberships at the same prices it pays $419.23. The gap is real and it is the whole reason Kick exists.

What the calculator cannot show is the other half of the decision: what a subscriber is worth on a platform with fewer viewers, what the payout threshold and schedule are, and whether the audience will follow. Treat the comparison as the ceiling of what the split is worth, not a forecast.

Reading your payout statement

Payout schedules differ and change, so treat these as shapes rather than promises: Twitch accumulates a balance and pays out monthly once it passes a minimum, with a longer tail for the first payment; YouTube pays through AdSense once the balance clears $100; Kick advertises faster, more frequent payouts. The number that matters is the net in your bank, and the statement breaks it into gross revenue, platform share, any store fees and withholding. If a line does not match this calculator, the statement is right and the assumptions here are wrong — the most common cause is a subscription price that differs by region, because Twitch and YouTube price subs in local currency and the dollar equivalent moves.

Frequently asked questions

How much is 1,000 Bits worth?

$10 to the streamer, one cent per Bit, before withholding. A viewer typically pays $12 to $15 for those Bits depending on how they bought them and in which country. Bits exist only on Twitch, so in the cross-platform comparison only the Twitch bar includes them.

Why does a Tier 1 sub pay $2.50 and not $4.99?

Because Twitch keeps half of the net subscription price. On the web that is 50 percent of $4.99, so $2.495. On a phone the store fee comes first: $4.99 less 30 percent is $3.49, half of which is about $1.75. Partner Plus changes the split, not the store fee.

Do I pay tax twice if my country also taxes the income?

Not twice on the same money, in principle. US withholding is creditable against the tax you owe at home under most treaties, so you declare the gross foreign income and claim the withheld amount as a credit. Keep the annual tax summary the platform issues. This is general information, not tax advice for your situation.

Is it better to ask viewers to subscribe on the web?

For you, yes: the same $4.99 pays about 43 percent more when the store is not involved. For the viewer it is the same price and one extra step, which is why many creators put the link in the stream description rather than arguing about it on air.

What about gifted subs and Prime subs?

Gifted subs pay the same share as a normal sub of that tier. Prime subs are a Twitch-specific product that pays the streamer roughly the same as a Tier 1 sub even though the viewer pays through their Prime membership. Enter them as Tier 1 subs; the difference is a couple of cents.

Does this calculator include ad revenue?

No. Ad revenue is a separate CPM-based calculation that depends on impressions, region and the ad fill rate, and it is reported separately on every platform. Add it yourself if you want a full picture of a month.

Before you count the money

Use last month's real numbers rather than a projection — the payout statement has every figure this page asks for. Then run the same month through the other platforms: the difference between a 50 percent and a 95 percent split is large enough that it should be a deliberate choice rather than an accident of where the audience happened to gather. Finally, keep the withholding rate current. It is set by forms you filed once, and a treaty rate that expired or a form that was never submitted quietly takes a third of every payout.
