What eDPI means, and what it does not

eDPI — “effective DPI” — is mouse DPI multiplied by in-game sensitivity. If your mouse is set to 800 DPI and the game is set to 0.35, your eDPI is 280. The point of the number is comparison: two players on different DPI settings can be ranked by a single figure, so a 1600 DPI player at sensitivity 0.175 and an 800 DPI player at 0.35 both come out at eDPI 280 and will feel the same camera speed in that game.

What eDPI does not do is travel between games. Each title converts mouse counts to camera degrees with its own constant, called yaw, and those constants differ by a factor of more than ten. Valorant turns 0.07 degrees per count at sensitivity 1; CS2 turns 0.022. That is why an eDPI of 280 in Valorant is a medium-speed aim and an eDPI of 280 in CS2 is a slow, deliberate one. Any converter that copies an eDPI number straight from one game to another is wrong by design. This page does the conversion properly, through the physical measurement underneath both: the distance your mouse has to travel for the camera to complete a full 360° turn.

The formula behind the converter

Camera rotation per mouse count is sensitivity × yaw degrees. To turn a full circle you need 360 ÷ (sensitivity × yaw) counts. Divide by DPI to get inches, multiply by 2.54 to get centimetres.

degrees per count = sensitivity × yaw counts per 360° = 360 ÷ (sensitivity × yaw) inches per 360° = counts per 360° ÷ DPI centimetres /360° = inches per 360° × 2.54 eDPI = DPI × sensitivity

Going the other way, from a 360° distance back to a sensitivity, is the same equation rearranged:

counts per 360° = (cm per 360° ÷ 2.54) × DPI sensitivity = 360 ÷ (counts per 360° × yaw)

Because yaw and DPI both cancel out of the intermediate step, the cross-game table is really just “keep counts-per-360 constant, then solve for each game’s yaw”. That is why the table is consistent even if you change DPI: the sensitivities shift, but the centimetres never do.

Yaw reference values used here

These are the values the community converters use, and they are the defaults in the advanced panel so you can override one if a patch changes it.

  • Valorant — 0.07. The largest yaw of the group, so a Valorant sensitivity number is small for the same physical speed.
  • CS2 — 0.022. Inherited from the Source engine, and shared by Apex Legends.
  • Apex Legends — 0.022. Identical to CS2, which is convenient: Apex and CS2 sensitivities are interchangeable at the same DPI.
  • Overwatch 2 — 0.0066. Overwatch numbers run much higher than CS2 for the same feel, typically around 3.3× the CS2 value.
  • Fortnite — 0.005555. The smallest yaw in this set apart from Siege, so Fortnite sensitivities look large.
  • Call of Duty — 0.0066. Matches Overwatch 2 in these references.
  • Rainbow Six Siege — 0.00572958. Slightly larger than Fortnite’s yaw, and note that Siege also has a separate ADS multiplier that this page does not model.

Two caveats. First, games sometimes apply an acceleration or smoothing curve that a pure yaw constant cannot capture; most competitive shooters now ship with raw input and no acceleration by default, which is what these numbers assume. Second, some titles have separate hip-fire and aim-down-sights sensitivities. The converter works in hip-fire units; copy the resulting number into hip-fire and scale your ADS setting by the same ratio if you want the aim-down-sights feel to follow.

Worked example: Valorant to CS2

800 DPI, Valorant 0.35

eDPI = 800 × 0.35 = 280. Degrees per count = 0.35 × 0.07 = 0.0245, so counts per 360° = 360 ÷ 0.0245 = 14,693.9. That is 18.367 inches of mouse travel, or 46.65 cm.

In CS2 with yaw 0.022, the sensitivity that gives the same 14,693.9 counts is 360 ÷ (14,693.9 × 0.022) = 1.114, which is an eDPI of about 891. Notice how far apart the eDPI numbers are — 280 versus 891 — for what is literally the same physical movement. This is the single most common source of confusion when players switch games.

Worked example: 1600 DPI in CS2

A faster mouse setting

1600 DPI at CS2 sensitivity 1.0 gives an eDPI of 1,600 and counts per 360° of 360 ÷ 0.022 = 16,363.6. Divided by 1600 DPI that is 10.227 inches, or 25.98 cm per full turn — a fairly quick tactical-shooter setting, on the high end of what most CS2 players use.

To make the same player comfortable in Valorant: 360 ÷ (16,363.6 × 0.07) = 0.314 at 1600 DPI. Same 25.98 cm, different number on the slider.

Worked example: starting from 30 cm/360°

Working backwards from distance

Suppose you measure 30 cm for a full turn at 800 DPI and want to play Overwatch 2. Counts per 360° = (30 ÷ 2.54) × 800 = 9,448.8. Overwatch yaw is 0.0066, so sensitivity = 360 ÷ (9,448.8 × 0.0066) = 5.773. In CS2 the same 30 cm is 360 ÷ (9,448.8 × 0.022) = 1.733.

This is the cleanest way to move between games: measure once with a ruler on the desk, then let the converter fill in every title.

Finding your cm/360 in the first place

Open a game, stand somewhere with a landmark in front of you, and put the mouse at the left edge of your mousepad. Turn until the crosshair has rotated exactly 180 degrees back to a wall you remember, then double the distance you moved. A tape measure across the desk is more accurate than guessing. Alternatively, set the sensitivity so that a full swipe across your pad is a 360° turn, then measure the pad’s usable width.

The number that suits you is personal, but the range is not mysterious. Very high sensitivity — under 15 cm per 360° — is mostly wrist and fingertip movement and suits players with small desks or fast-twitch games. The 15–25 cm band is common among controller-to-mouse converts and battle-royale players who need fast turns. The 25–35 cm band is the broad middle where most Overwatch and Apex players sit. 35–50 cm is tactical-shooter territory, where CS2 and Valorant players trade turning speed for precision, and beyond 50 cm you are into full arm aim, wide desk, low-DPI setups.

There is no advantage in copying a professional’s number exactly. Pros are separated by reaction time, crosshair placement and game sense, not by two centimetres of sensitivity. What matters is consistency: pick a setting, give it at least two weeks, and stop adjusting it after every bad match.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good eDPI?

There is no universal good value, and it is game-specific. In Valorant, most players land between 200 and 400 eDPI; in CS2, between 600 and 1,200; in Overwatch 2 and Apex, higher. Convert through cm/360 rather than comparing raw eDPI between titles.

Why is my CS2 sensitivity twice my Valorant sensitivity?

Because Valorant’s yaw is 0.07 and CS2’s is 0.022, a ratio of about 3.18. The CS2 number is roughly three times the Valorant number for the same physical mouse speed. If yours is not, check that you are comparing at the same DPI.

Does DPI change the feel if I keep eDPI the same?

In theory no, and in practice almost. Higher DPI gives finer angular steps per count, so the camera can feel slightly smoother, and very high DPI settings can introduce sensor smoothing on some mice. Most players are fine anywhere between 400 and 3,200 DPI. What you should avoid is a DPI so low that the game’s count resolution becomes coarse, or so high that the mouse reports interpolated values.

Do these conversions work for aim-down-sights sensitivity?

Not directly. Titles with separate ADS multipliers scale the aim speed by a factor this page does not model. Convert your hip-fire sensitivity, then set the ADS multiplier so that your aim-down-sights cm/360 matches what you used in the previous game — usually by testing a slow 180° turn and measuring it.

Why does Fortnite sensitivity look so high in the table?

Fortnite’s yaw is small (about 0.005555), so more sensitivity points are needed per degree of rotation. A Fortnite sensitivity of 8 percent is not “eight times faster” than a Valorant 1.0 — the two use different scales entirely.

Is mouse acceleration included?

No. These formulas assume raw input with acceleration disabled, which is the default in competitive shooters and the standard way to compare. If your mouse software or Windows pointer settings add acceleration, the same in-game number will not produce a consistent 360° distance.

Using the result well

Write your cm/360 down once and keep it: it is the only sensitivity figure that survives a game switch, a new mouse or a DPI change. When you try a new title, enter that distance and let the converter produce the starting sensitivity, then spend a session or two adjusting by a few percent rather than starting from zero. And if a game patch ever changes its sensitivity scale, the yaw override in the advanced panel is there so you can correct the converter without waiting for someone else to update a table.

Sensitivity values are community reference figures and can change with game updates. The converter is a calculation aid; verify against your own measured 360° distance.
