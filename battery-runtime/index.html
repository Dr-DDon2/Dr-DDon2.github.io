What this calculator answers

“How long will it run?” is the only question anyone asks about a battery, and it is the question a battery label answers worst. A 100Ah 12V battery does not give you 100Ah, and it certainly does not give you 1,200Wh at the socket. Three things eat into the number before the first device is even switched on: the chemistry will not let you use the whole pack, the inverter loses 10 to 15 percent converting DC to AC, and in a lead-acid battery the usable capacity falls as you draw harder. This page applies all three and reports the time you will actually get — plus the pack size you would need to reach a target number of hours.

It is built for the two situations where the arithmetic matters most: sizing a small UPS for a router, a NAS or a CPAP that must ride out an outage, and sizing a solar or camper battery bank where every extra amp-hour is money and weight. Both come down to the same two multiplications.

The four numbers behind the runtime

load current (A) = watts ÷ (inverter efficiency × pack voltage) usable capacity = rated Ah × depth of discharge × Peukert factor runtime (h) = usable capacity ÷ load current Peukert factor = (rated Ah ÷ 20) ÷ load current, raised to (k − 1) [lead-acid only]

The first line converts the load into the current the battery actually sees. A 100W load on a 12V pack through a 90 percent inverter pulls 100 ÷ (0.9 × 12) = 9.26 amps, not 8.33. On a 24V pack the same load pulls 4.63 amps, which is why doubling the voltage halves the current and lets you use thinner cable and a smaller fuse for the same power.

The second line is the part people skip. Depth of discharge is the share of the pack a healthy install should use on a regular basis: 90 percent for LiFePO4, 80 for lithium-ion NMC, and half for any lead-acid chemistry if you want the battery to reach its advertised cycle count. A 100Ah AGM really gives you 50Ah of useful energy, not 100.

The Peukert factor is the odd one. A lead-acid battery's capacity is quoted at a slow 20-hour discharge. Pull harder and the chemistry loses more energy to internal resistance than the rating suggests. The exponent k describes how bad that is: about 1.15 for AGM and gel, 1.25 for flooded cells, and effectively 1.0 for lithium, which is why the checkbox does nothing for a lithium pack. At a 9 amp draw from a 100Ah AGM, the factor is 0.91 — you lose another 9 percent on top of the 50 percent depth-of-discharge cut.

Worked example: a router and an ONT through an outage

Small load, long time

A Wi-Fi router draws 12W and the fibre ONT another 8W, so the load is 20W. On a 12V 100Ah LiFePO4 pack with a 90 percent inverter, the current is 20 ÷ (0.9 × 12) = 1.85A and the usable capacity is 90Ah. Runtime is 90 ÷ 1.85 = 48.6 hours — two full days of internet from a pack the size of a car battery.

Add the inverter's 15W idle draw and the load nearly doubles to 35W: 7 minutes per amp-hour becomes about 26 hours. This is the single most common surprise in small backup systems. If the load is small and always DC, use a DC-DC converter or a 12V-native router rather than an inverter, and set the efficiency to about 95 percent.

Worked example: the same 100Ah in AGM

Chemistry is worth more than capacity

Take the 100Ah 12V pack and make it AGM. The depth of discharge drops from 90 to 50 percent, so usable capacity is 50Ah instead of 90. At a 100W load (9.26A), the Peukert exponent of 1.15 trims that to 45.6Ah. Runtime is 45.6 ÷ 9.26 = 4.9 hours.

The LiFePO4 version of the identical pack runs 9.7 hours at the same load. Twice the usable time from the same label, and roughly four to six times the cycle life: 3,000 cycles to 80 percent capacity for LiFePO4 against 400 to 500 for AGM. On a cost-per-usable-amp-hour basis the lithium pack usually wins even at twice the sticker price, which is why the comparison bar on this page is the most useful thing on it.

Worked example: sizing a CPAP for a night

Working backwards from the hours

A CPAP without the humidifier draws about 40W. On 12V through a 90 percent inverter that is 3.70A. Eight hours needs 29.6Ah of delivered energy, and at 90 percent depth of discharge the nameplate must be 32.9Ah. A 50Ah LiFePO4 pack gives 12.2 hours — a night plus a cushion for a second night if the humidifier stays off.

Run the same sum for a 24 hour target and the answer is 98.8Ah, which is why the “hours of backup wanted” field exists: it turns the calculator from a what-if into a shopping list. Keep 20 percent in reserve on top of whatever it says, because cold cells lose capacity and an ageing pack loses more.

Chemistry, depth of discharge and cycle life

  • LiFePO4: 90 percent usable, about 3,000 cycles, flat voltage curve, no maintenance, safe chemistry. The default choice for a new build.
  • Lithium-ion NMC: 80 percent usable, about 800 cycles, lighter per watt-hour than LiFePO4 but less tolerant of abuse and more expensive to insure.
  • AGM and gel: 50 percent usable, 400 to 500 cycles, sealed and maintenance-free, cheap to buy and heavy to carry. Accepts a high charge current, which suits a vehicle alternator.
  • Flooded lead-acid: 50 percent usable, about 400 cycles, cheapest per amp-hour and the most forgiving of overcharge — but it needs water checks and ventilation.

All four also lose capacity in the cold: figure 20 percent down at freezing for lead-acid and 10 to 15 percent for lithium, which is why a pack rated for a mild garage can come up short in an unheated shed in January.

Frequently asked questions

How long will a 100Ah battery run a 100W load?

A 12V 100Ah LiFePO4 pack runs about 9 hours 43 minutes through a 90 percent efficient inverter. In AGM the same pack gives roughly 4 hours 55 minutes, and in flooded lead-acid a little less than that. On a 24V 100Ah pack the runtime doubles, because the energy is twice as much for the same amp-hour label.

Is Ah or Wh the better way to compare batteries?

Watt-hours, always. Amp-hours only mean something alongside a voltage: a 100Ah 12V pack holds 1,200Wh, and a 100Ah 24V pack holds 2,400Wh. Solar batteries are increasingly sold in Wh or kWh for exactly this reason. Enter whichever number is printed and the calculator converts.

Why does the runtime drop so much as the load grows?

Two reasons. Runtime is inverse to the current, so doubling the load halves the time — that part is simple arithmetic. Then in lead-acid the Peukert effect reduces usable capacity at higher currents, so the time falls faster than the load rises. A 100Ah AGM gives about 4.9 hours at 100W but only about 1.4 hours at 300W, not the 1.6 hours that plain arithmetic would suggest.

Should I use the inverter's rated wattage instead of the equipment draw?

No. Size the inverter on the surge and the load on the running watts. A 1000W inverter running a 20W load still draws its own idle power, and reporting the inverter's rating as the load would understate the runtime by a factor of fifty.

How does temperature change the answer?

A lead-acid battery at 0°C delivers roughly 80 percent of its 25°C capacity, and at −10°C closer to 70. Lithium loses less, around 10 to 15 percent at freezing, but should not be charged below 0°C. If the pack lives in an unheated space, take 20 percent off the result for planning.

Can I connect two batteries to double the runtime?

In parallel you add the amp-hours at the same voltage; in series you add the voltage at the same amp-hours. Either way the watt-hours double, so the runtime doubles, provided both batteries are the same chemistry, age and state of charge. Mixing an old and a new battery in parallel drags the new one down to the old one's behaviour.

Before you buy

Measure the load with a plug-in energy meter rather than trusting a label: a “100W” TV often draws 60, and a “35W” NAS with four drives can peak at 70 on spin-up. Then decide how much of the outage you actually need to cover, because the difference between four hours and twenty-four is usually three times the battery. And check the charge side before the battery side — a pack that cannot be recharged during a multi-day outage is a one-shot resource, whatever its runtime says.

This calculator provides estimates for planning. Real runtime depends on the age, temperature and health of the pack, the inverter's actual efficiency curve and the true duty cycle of each load. Verify against the manufacturer's discharge data before relying on a battery for medical equipment.
