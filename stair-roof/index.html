What this page calculates

Two of the most common framing problems are the same problem wearing different clothes: you know the vertical distance and you need the number of pieces, the size of each piece and the length of the diagonal that carries them. On a stair that diagonal is the stringer. On a roof it is the rafter. Both are solved with the same right triangle, and both are easy to get wrong by an eighth of an inch that turns into a visible error at the top of the flight or the end of the ridge.

The stair half of this page works from a single measurement — the total rise from the finished lower floor to the finished upper floor. From that it picks the number of risers, divides the rise evenly, works out how many treads that leaves, multiplies the tread depth by the tread count to get the total run, and takes the diagonal to give the stringer length. It then compares the result against the residential stair limits in the International Residential Code, section R311.7, and tells you which limit, if any, the design breaks.

The roof half converts a pitch written the way carpenters write it — so many inches of rise for every 12 inches of horizontal run — into the numbers a square, a level and a saw actually use: the angle in degrees, the slope as a percentage, and the multipliers that give rafter lengths for any run.

The stair formulas, line by line

Nothing here is a rule of thumb hiding a fudge factor. Each output is a single line of arithmetic, and you can run all of it on a calculator to check the page.

risers = round(total rise ÷ target riser) minimum 2 riser height = total rise ÷ risers treads = risers − 1 total run = treads × tread depth stringer = √(total rise² + total run²) stair angle = atan(total rise ÷ total run) stock length = stringer + 12 in (plumb cuts at both ends)

The riser count is the only place a choice is made, and the choice is rounding. A 108 inch rise with a 7.5 inch target gives 14.4, which rounds to 14 risers, so each riser is 108 ÷ 14 = 7.714 inches. Rounding up to 15 would give 7.2 inch risers — legal, but a shallower, longer stair that often will not fit the space. Rounding down to 13 would give 8.3 inch risers, which breaks the code limit, so the calculator would tell you.

Note that a stair with 14 risers has 13 treads. The top riser lands on the upper floor, and the upper floor is a tread you do not build. That off-by-one is the most common error in a hand-drawn stair plan, and it is worth checking against the total run: 13 treads at 10.5 inches is 136.5 inches of horizontal travel, which for a 108 inch rise is a 38.35 degree stair — steep but normal for a deck or a basement.

The code limits this page checks

The 2021 International Residential Code sets the following for a residential stairway. Commercial work follows the International Building Code, which is stricter about some of these and adds requirements the IRC does not have, so a shop or an office stair needs a different check.

  • Riser height, R311.7.5.1: maximum 7-3/4 inches (196 mm). There is no minimum in the code, but anything under about 4 inches reads as a trip hazard in practice.
  • Tread depth, R311.7.5.2: minimum 10 inches (254 mm) measured horizontally between the nosings of adjacent treads.
  • Uniformity, R311.7.5: the largest riser in a flight may be no more than 3/8 inch taller than the smallest. Dividing the total rise evenly is the simplest way to satisfy this, and it is why the calculator does it that way.
  • Stairway width, R311.7.1: minimum 36 inches of clear width above the handrail height.
  • Nosing, R311.7.5.3: where the tread is less than 11 inches deep, the nosing projects between 3/4 inch and 1-1/4 inches past the riser below it, with a radius no sharper than 9/16 inch.
  • Headroom, R311.7.5: 6 ft 8 in measured vertically from the nosing line. A stair that passes under a floor joist, a beam or the upper landing is where this fails, and no calculator can see it for you.
  • Handrail, R311.7.8: required on at least one side of a stair with four or more risers, 34 to 38 inches above the nosing line.

A pass on all four checks in the table is a necessary condition, not a permit. Landing size, guard height, the slope of the ground at the bottom and the door swing at the top are all outside what a two-number calculator can know.

Worked example: a 9 ft deck stair

Deck at 108 inches above grade

Total rise 108 in, target riser 7.5 in. 108 ÷ 7.5 = 14.4, so 14 risers at exactly 7-3/4 in — right at the code limit but not over it. 13 treads at 10.5 in give a total run of 136.5 in, or 11 ft 4-1/2 in of horizontal travel.

The stringer is √(108² + 136.5²) = √(11,664 + 18,632.25) = 174.06 in, which is 14 ft 6 in. Add a foot for the plumb cuts and buy 15 ft 6 in — in practice a 16 ft 2×12, because lumber yards sell in even lengths. The stair angle is atan(108 ÷ 136.5) = 38.35°, which is a comfortable outdoor stair, not a ladder and not a ramp.

Because the tread is 10.5 in, the nosing rule applies and a 1 in projection is correct. Two stringers carry the width if the stair is under about 3 ft; at 36 in clear width, three stringers at 16 in centres is the safer framing for a deck stair that will see a crowd.

Worked example: a metric stair to a mezzanine

2,500 mm rise, working in millimetres

Switch the unit to mm and enter 2,500. That is 98.43 in. Divided by a 7.5 in target, 2,500 mm ÷ 190.5 mm = 13.12, so 13 risers at 192.3 mm each — under the 196 mm limit. 12 treads at 267 mm (10.5 in) give a 3,200 mm run and a 4,058 mm stringer, and the angle is 38.0°.

Metric lumber is usually sold as 45 × 250 mm for a stringer, which gives 250 mm of depth for the tread-and-riser cut. A 267 mm tread plus a 192 mm riser is 459 mm of cut across the board — far more than 250 mm — so the stringer must be cut with the tread cantilevered, or the stair built with a housed-stringer design instead of a notched one. This is where a metric build diverges from the American pattern: check the actual board before trusting the cut list.

Worked example: a 6:12 gable roof

10 ft run, 1.5 ft overhang

A 6:12 pitch rises 6 inches per foot of run, so the angle is atan(6 ÷ 12) = 26.57° and the slope is 50 percent. The common rafter multiplier is √(144 + 36) ÷ 12 = 1.1180, and the hip multiplier is √(288 + 36) ÷ 12 = 1.5000.

With a 10 ft run and a 1.5 ft overhang, the rafter run is 11.5 ft. The common rafter is 11.5 × 1.1180 = 12.86 ft, and the same run on the diagonal is 11.5 × 1.5000 = 17.25 ft for a hip or valley. The rise over the wall line is 10 × 0.5 = 5 ft; over the full rafter run, including the tail, it is 5.75 ft.

Rafters are bought in even lengths and cut with a ridge reduction, so a 12.86 ft rafter comes out of a 14 ft board with the plumb and birdsmouth cuts allowed for. If the roof is a hip, the 17.25 ft hip rafter needs a 20 ft board, and the 45° plan angle means the hip sits √2 times as far across the plan as a common rafter travels — which is exactly what the larger multiplier already accounts for.

Why the rafter multipliers look the way they do

Every multiplier is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are 12 inches of run and the pitch in inches of rise. For a 6:12 roof that is √(12² + 6²) = 13.416 inches of rafter for every 12 inches of run; divide by 12 and you get 1.118. The hip and valley multiplier uses √(12² + 12² + pitch²) instead, because a hip travels diagonally across the plan: it covers 12 inches of run in one direction and 12 in the other while climbing the same pitch. That is where the 288 comes from — 144 + 144.

These are the numbers printed on the little blue card that comes with a framing square, and they have not changed in a century because the geometry has not changed. The table on this page lists six of the most common pitches so you can sanity-check a roof before committing to a cut list, and the inputs above will do any pitch from 1:12 to 24:12.

Frequently asked questions

How many stairs do I need for a 9 foot rise?

Fourteen risers at 7-3/4 inches each, with 13 treads. That works out of 108 ÷ 7.5 = 14.4 rounded to 14. If you prefer a shallower stair with a 7 inch riser, you get 108 ÷ 7 = 15.4, so 15 risers at 7.2 inches and 14 treads — a longer stair by about a foot of run.

Can I use a 2×10 for a stringer?

Usually not. A 2×10 is 9-1/4 inches wide, and the deeper the tread and the taller the riser, the more of that width the notch removes. A normal stair with a 10 inch tread and a 7-1/2 inch riser wants the full 11-1/4 inches of a 2×12. Shallow, long stairs over a low rise can sometimes be cut from a 2×10 — the number that decides it is the throat, the wood left under the notches, not the outside dimension of the board.

What is the throat of a stair stringer and why does it matter?

The throat is the wood left between the inside corner of the notch and the far edge of the board, measured perpendicular to the slope: the narrowest cross-section the stringer has under the steps. The code does not set a number, but 3-1/2 inches is the accepted minimum for a 2×12 and many framers will not go below 5. A short throat is where stringers crack, so check it on the first stringer before using it as a template.

Is a 38 degree stair too steep?

It is a normal residential stair. The familiar comfortable range is roughly 30 to 37 degrees, and a 7-3/4 inch riser with a 10 inch tread lands at about 37.8 degrees. Steeper than 40 degrees starts to feel like a ship's ladder, and the code limit on riser height with a 10 inch minimum tread caps the practical maximum at about 37.8 degrees for a legal IRC stair.

How do I measure the total rise?

From the finished surface at the bottom to the finished surface at the top — not from the subfloor, and not from the ground if there will be a landing or a slab poured there later. A 1 inch error in the total rise is spread across every riser, so it changes each step by less than a tenth of an inch, but it changes the top step by the whole inch, which is the one people trip on.

Does pitch mean the same thing as slope?

In roofing, yes in spirit but not in units. Pitch is rise over 12 of run, written 6:12. Slope is the same relationship as a percentage, so a 6:12 is a 50 percent slope. An angle in degrees is what a saw or a digital level reads. All three describe one triangle, which is why this page shows all three for every pitch you enter — and why a roof quoted as "26.57 degrees" and "6:12" is the same roof.

Before you cut

Write the total rise and total run on the board before you mark anything, and mark the first stringer as a template for the rest — every stringer in a flight has to be identical, and the only reliable way to guarantee that is to cut one and trace it. Check the notch depth against the actual width of the board you bought rather than its nominal size, because a 2×12 is 11-1/4 inches and the difference is what decides whether the stringer survives its first winter. For a roof, mark the pitch on the rafter with a square and confirm the first two rafters sit at the same height at the ridge before cutting the rest of the set.

This calculator provides estimates for planning. Local amendments, commercial codes and the actual board you buy can all change the answer. Verify against the code your inspector enforces and measure the real materials before cutting.
