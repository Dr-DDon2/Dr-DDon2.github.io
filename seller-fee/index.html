What this calculator actually answers

Most sellers price a product by doubling the material cost and then feel disappointed at the end of the month. The gap between “I sold it for $45” and “I made $22” is made of five or six small deductions that each look harmless on their own. This page adds them up per order, in the same order the platforms do: percentage fees on the gross amount, a fixed processing charge, listing costs, advertising, and finally the real cost of the product and the parcel.

The output is deliberately split in three places. The headline number is net profit per order. Under it, gross revenue, total fees with their effective percentage, ad spend and break-even price. Below that, a donut and a line-by-line list so you can see which cost is eating the margin — very often it is shipping, not the platform.

Everything is per order and before income tax. Sales tax and VAT are collected from the buyer and passed through, so they are not part of your revenue and are not modelled here.

How marketplace money flows

A buyer pays the item price plus whatever shipping you charge. That sum is gross revenue, and it is the number every percentage fee is calculated from. The platform deducts its transaction or referral fee, the payment processor deducts its percentage and a fixed amount, and Etsy adds a listing fee of 20 cents per listing, refreshed every four months whether or not the item sells.

What remains is not profit. You still have to pay for the product, the packaging and the postage label. A seller who charges $5 shipping but pays $9 for postage is losing $4 on every order before a single fee is applied, and the calculator shows that plainly: the “actual shipping you pay” line sits next to the shipping charged so the subsidy is visible.

Advertising is the last and most variable cost. On Etsy, offsite ads charge 15 percent of the order total when an outside advertisement brings the sale — 12 percent for shops that have sold more than $10,000 in the previous 365 days. On TikTok Shop, sellers typically pay an affiliate commission to the creator whose video sold the item, plus whatever they spend on Shop Ads. Both are modelled here as a percentage or a fixed amount per order, or as a ROAS target.

Etsy fees in detail

Etsy’s structure has four parts that matter for a single order:

  • Transaction fee, 6.5 percent. Charged on the item price plus the shipping you charge the buyer, and on gift wrap if you offer it. It does not come off the shipping cost you pay.
  • Payment processing, about 3 percent plus $0.25 for US shops on Etsy Payments. The exact rate depends on your shop’s country; several European countries use 4 percent plus a fixed local amount.
  • Listing fee, $0.20. One per listing, and again every time the listing renews after four months, or when it sells and auto-renews. For a single order it is a small fixed cost, but a shop with 300 listings pays $60 a year whether it sells or not.
  • Offsite ads, 15 percent of the order total, only when the sale came from an offsite ad. It can be switched off only while the shop is under the $10,000 threshold.

Add the first three together and a typical US order loses roughly 9.5 percent of gross revenue plus 45 cents. On a $45 order with no shipping charged, that is about $4.73, which is 10.5 percent — the fixed amount is a bigger share of a small order than of a large one. This is why cheap items struggle on Etsy: a $6 sticker set pays the same 45 cents as a $60 print.

TikTok Shop fees in detail

TikTok Shop’s US fee stack looks simpler and often is not:

  • Referral fee, 6 percent for most categories (some categories are higher). It applies to the order value, so shipping charged is usually inside the base.
  • Payment processing, about 2.9 percent plus $0.30.
  • Affiliate commission. Set by the seller per product, commonly 10 to 20 percent for beauty, home and apparel. It is paid to the creator and is charged on the item price, not on shipping.
  • Shop Ads and promotions. Optional, but in a video-first marketplace they are how most sellers get discovered.

The affiliate commission is the line sellers forget. A 10 percent commission on top of a 6 percent referral fee and 2.9 percent processing means roughly 19 percent of revenue disappears before the product cost, and it is usually the difference between a healthy launch and a product that only looks popular.

The formulas, spelled out

All values are per order. Fee percentages are converted to decimals inside the calculator.

revenue = item price + shipping charged platform fee = revenue × platform % payment fee = revenue × payment % + fixed fee offsite fee = revenue × offsite % (Etsy offsite ads only) affiliate fee = item price × affiliate % listing = listing fee ad spend = fixed $ | revenue × % | revenue ÷ ROAS net profit = revenue − platform − payment − offsite − affiliate − listing − ad spend − product cost − shipping cost margin % = net profit ÷ revenue × 100

Break-even is the item price where net profit equals exactly zero, holding shipping charged and every cost constant. Solving the profit equation for the price gives:

k = 1 − (platform % + payment % + offsite %) − ad% break-even price = (product cost + shipping cost + listing + fixed fee + fixed ad spend − shipping charged × k) ÷ k

If k is zero or negative, percentage costs already consume all of the revenue and no price can break even; the calculator says so instead of printing a meaningless number. If the result is zero or negative, the shipping you charge already covers the costs, which is a good problem to have.

Worked example: a $45 Etsy print, no ads

The baseline order

Price $45, free shipping to the buyer, $6 actually spent on the label, $12 in materials and packaging. Transaction fee 6.5 percent of $45 is $2.93. Payment processing is 3 percent ($1.35) plus $0.25 = $1.60. The listing fee is $0.20.

Total fees: $4.73, or 10.5 percent of revenue. Net profit = 45 − 4.73 − 12 − 6 = $22.28, a margin of 49.5 percent. Healthy, but note that shipping and materials together are twice the platform’s cut. If the parcel actually costs $9, profit falls to $19.28 and the margin to 42.8 percent.

Worked example: a $29.99 TikTok Shop order

Affiliate commission plus ads

Price $29.99, buyer pays $4.99 shipping, you pay $6.50 for the label, product cost $9, affiliate commission 10 percent, and $5 of Shop Ads attributed to this order.

Revenue is $34.98. Referral fee at 6 percent is $2.10. Affiliate commission at 10 percent of the item price is $3.00. Payment processing 2.9 percent of $34.98 plus $0.30 is $1.31. Ad spend $5.00.

Net profit = 34.98 − 2.10 − 3.00 − 1.31 − 5.00 − 9.00 − 6.50 = $8.07, a margin of 23.1 percent. Halve the affiliate commission to 5 percent and profit rises to $9.57; drop the ads entirely and it reaches $13.07. This is the shape of most TikTok Shop economics: the product is fine, the acquisition cost decides.

Worked example: finding a break-even price

What price stops the bleeding?

An Etsy seller offers free shipping on a $20 item. The parcel costs $6 to send, materials are $15, and 10 percent of revenue goes to ads. Fees are the Etsy defaults: 6.5 percent transaction, 3 percent plus $0.25 processing, $0.20 listing.

On a $20 sale the order brings in $20, pays $1.30 in transaction fees, $0.85 in processing, $2.00 in ads and $0.20 in listing fees. Add $15 of product and $6 of postage and the order costs $25.35 to deliver — a loss of $5.35 on every sale.

Solving for zero profit gives a break-even price of $26.65. At $26.65 the order earns nothing at all; every dollar below that is a dollar lost. Raising the price to $29.99 turns the same order into a $2.69 profit (9 percent). That gap between $20 and $29.99 is exactly the difference between a shop that looks busy and a shop that makes money, and it is invisible until every fee and every cost is on one line.

Margin, markup and the number that matters

Markup is profit divided by cost; margin is profit divided by revenue. A product that costs $10 and sells for $20 has a 100 percent markup but only a 50 percent margin. After $4 of fees and $5 of shipping it has a markup of just 10 percent and a margin of 5 percent, which is why “I double my costs” is not a pricing strategy on a marketplace that charges for shipping and processing.

As a rule of thumb for Etsy, price at 2.6 to 3 times your landed cost (materials plus packaging plus postage) if you want a 40 percent margin after fees with no ads. For TikTok Shop with a 10 percent affiliate commission, push closer to 3.2 times. And treat advertising as a separate budget line: if the ad spend per order exceeds the profit per order without ads, you are buying revenue, not earning it.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage does Etsy take in total?

For a typical US order paid through Etsy Payments, budget around 9.5 percent of the order total plus about 45 cents: 6.5 percent transaction, 3 percent plus $0.25 processing, and a 20 cent listing. Offsite ads add 15 percent when they generate the sale. Rates vary by country and by shop, so confirm them in your payment settings.

Does Etsy charge fees on shipping?

Yes. The transaction fee and the payment processing fee are both calculated on the item price plus the shipping you charge. Charging “free shipping” and building the cost into the price produces the same fee, because the fee follows the total either way.

Why is my profit lower than the platform’s own calculator?

Platform dashboards usually show the payout for an order, not the profit: they do not know your material cost, your packaging or the real postage you paid. They also tend to exclude ad spend booked elsewhere, and Etsy’s offsite ad fee is charged separately. This calculator deducts everything, which is why the number is lower and more honest.

Should I include my own labour in the product cost?

Yes, at least at a nominal hourly rate. A hand-made item that takes 45 minutes to produce and earns $6 of “profit” is paying you $8 an hour. Including labour usually reveals that the price needs to rise or the process needs to get faster.

What is a good margin for a marketplace product?

30 to 50 percent net is a comfortable target for handmade and small-batch goods. Below 20 percent, a single return, a damaged parcel or a supplier price increase wipes out several sales. Perishable, heavy or fragile items need a higher margin because the failure rate is higher.

How do returns affect these numbers?

A return usually refunds the buyer’s payment while the original fees are only partly reversed, and you may pay return shipping. If 5 percent of orders come back, your effective margin drops by roughly 5 percent of revenue plus the return postage. Add that as a fixed cost per order, or raise the price to cover it.

Do these fees include sales tax or VAT?

No. Sales tax and VAT are collected from the buyer and remitted by the platform, so they never become your revenue. Income tax on your profit is also not included — this is a per-order operating profit figure.

Keeping the numbers honest

Fee schedules are moving targets. Etsy has changed its payment rates by country more than once, TikTok Shop has adjusted referral fees by category as it entered new markets, and both add regulatory operating fees in some jurisdictions. Treat the defaults on this page as a starting point that is accurate enough to make a pricing decision, then open your seller dashboard once a quarter and correct the fields. The break-even number is the one to watch: as long as your price sits comfortably above it, a fee change moves your margin but does not sink the product.

This calculator provides estimates for planning, not accounting or tax advice. Fee rates, payment terms and advertising costs vary by country, category and account. Confirm current rates with the platform before you rely on the result.
