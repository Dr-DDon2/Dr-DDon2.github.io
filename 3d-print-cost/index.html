Why a 3D print costs more than the filament

Ask a hobbyist what a print costs and the answer is filament weight times price per gram. That number is usually wrong by a factor of two to four, and the gap is not mysterious: it is everything that happened around the filament. A multi-colour model throws away as much plastic in purge as it puts in the part. Supports are printed and then broken off. Some prints fail and the filament, the power and the machine hours are gone. The printer itself wears out. Electricity is small but real. And somebody had to load the spool, remove the print, remove the supports, pack it and ship it.

This calculator adds those in the order a real job accumulates them, then converts the total into a price that holds your margin after the marketplace takes its cut. It is built for people selling prints on Etsy or Shopify, and equally for people deciding whether it is cheaper to print a $12 bracket or drive to the store.

The cost formulas

cost per gram = spool price ÷ spool weight filament = (part + purge + support) × cost per gram energy = print hours × watts ÷ 1000 × price per kWh depreciation = (printer price ÷ life hours) × print hours failure factor = 1 ÷ (1 − failure rate) cost = (filament + energy + depreciation) × failure factor + hands-on time × your rate + packaging price = (cost + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − margin %) profit = price − price × fee % − fixed fee − cost

Two choices in that block are worth explaining. Failures gross up filament, power and machine time but not labour or packaging, because a failure costs another print, not another half hour of your attention and a second mailer. And the price formula divides by one minus the fees and margin rather than multiplying cost by a factor, because fees are charged on the selling price, not on the cost. That distinction is where most hobbyist pricing goes wrong.

Margin is not markup

A 30 percent margin means the profit is 30 percent of the price, so the price is cost ÷ 0.70 — a 43 percent markup. A 30 percent markup gives a 23 percent margin. On a $5 cost the difference is $7.14 against $6.50, and once a 9.5 percent fee is taken the markup version is barely above break-even. This calculator asks for the margin, because that is the number that tells you whether the business works: a 30 percent margin on $15 is $4.50 that has to cover your time, the odd refund, packaging you forgot and the tax you will owe.

Worked example: a two-colour 120 g part

The defaults on this page

A $25 spool of 1 kg filament is $0.025 per gram. The part weighs 120 g, the AMS flush for the colour changes is another 40 g and the supports 20 g, so the print consumes 180 g and $4.50 of filament. Eight hours at an average 120 W is 0.96 kWh, which at $0.16 is $0.15. A $600 printer with a 4,000 hour life costs $0.15 an hour, so $1.20 for the print. Ten minutes of your time at $15 an hour is $2.50.

Add a 5 percent failure rate and the material, power and machine costs rise from $5.85 to $6.16. Total cost $8.66. With a 30 percent margin and Etsy's 9.5 percent plus $0.45, the price is ($8.66 + $0.45) ÷ 0.605 = $15.06, and after the $1.88 fee the profit is $4.52. Notice that the purge and support are $1.50 of the $4.50 filament — a third of the plastic never reaches the customer.

Worked example: a small single-colour part

Where labour dominates

A 50 g bracket, no purge, no supports, two hours on a 100 W printer at $0.20 per kWh. Material is $1.00 on a $20 spool, electricity $0.04, depreciation on a $300 printer with a 3,000 hour life $0.20. That is $1.24 of machine cost and then ten minutes of hands-on time at $18 an hour adds $3.00 — nearly three quarters of the whole cost.

At a 40 percent margin with no marketplace fee the price is $4.24 ÷ 0.6 = $7.07. The lesson for small parts is that batching matters more than filament price: print ten at once, handle them in one session, and the labour per unit falls from $3.00 to well under a dollar. A printer that runs unattended overnight is only cheap if you stop paying yourself by the minute for each print.

AMS purge is the hidden cost of colour

A single-colour print wastes nothing to colour changes. Add a second colour and the printer must flush the nozzle at every transition: the old colour has to be pushed out before the new one arrives. The flush volume depends on the transition — white to black needs far more than black to grey — and on the slicer's settings. In Bambu Studio the flush volumes are editable per transition, and the default “auto” calculation is generous; users routinely find they can halve it without visible bleed.

On a model with a hundred colour changes, the total flush can be several times the model's own weight. This is why a 60 g multi-colour figurine can consume 200 g of filament, and why the cost per colour copy falls sharply when you print several at once — the purge per layer is shared across every object on the plate. Before pricing any multi-colour model, slice it, read the total filament weight in the preview, subtract the part weight, and put the difference in the purge field. If you use a purge tower or a wipe object, that material is in the total too.

Depreciation, and why hobbyists skip it

A printer is a consumable with a clock. Belts stretch, nozzles wear, the bed surface is scraped, bearings develop play, and eventually a board or a stepper fails. Dividing the purchase price by a realistic service life — 3,000 to 5,000 hours for a consumer machine that is maintained, less if it runs production hours — gives a cost per hour that belongs in every sold print. Ignoring it feels better because it delays the pain: the money leaves later, in one lump, when the printer is replaced out of what looked like profit.

Count maintenance parts separately if you want precision: a nozzle every few hundred hours, a build plate every year, a few belts. For most sellers, folding them into a slightly shorter life estimate is accurate enough.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to print 100 g of plastic?

Filament costs $2 to $3.50 at typical spool prices, and a 100 g print at 0.2 mm layers takes around 5 to 7 hours, which adds roughly $0.10 of electricity and $0.75 to $1.00 of machine depreciation. So a fair all-in figure before labour is $3 to $4.50. The number people quote, “a dollar of plastic”, describes the raw material only.

How do I find my purge weight?

Slice the model with your real colour assignments and look at the filament summary: the slicer reports total weight and, in most versions, the flush weight separately. If it does not, slice once in a single colour and subtract that weight from the multi-colour weight. Either way, the number includes the purge tower if you use one.

What is a realistic failure rate?

Five percent is a fair average for a dialled-in printer running a proven profile. First-layer problems, tall thin parts, ABS warping and overnight prints with no monitoring all push it higher; a design you have already printed a dozen times can be one or two percent. Use your own record: count the failed prints in your last thirty jobs and divide.

Should shipping be in the price?

Keep it separate. Charge shipping at cost, or offer free shipping with the postage and packaging already in the item price — but then raise the fixed fee field to cover the label, because a marketplace fee applies to the total the buyer pays. This calculator prices the item, not the delivery.

Is it cheaper to print a part or buy it?

For a one-off small part, buying usually wins once you count your design time — a $12 bracket is cheaper than six hours of CAD and a failed print. Printing wins when the part does not exist commercially, when you need five of them, or when the geometry is bespoke. Use the cost figure the calculator gives you, then add your design hours at your own rate before deciding.

What about the electricity cost of a drying box or a filament dryer?

Add it to the packaging or extra cost field, or divide it across the prints that came from the spool. A dryer running two hours at 50 W is 0.1 kWh — pennies per spool — but a heated chamber or an enclosure heater during an eight hour ABS print can double the energy figure, so raise the average printer draw instead.

Before you list it

Slice the real model with the real colours and read the total filament weight, not the part weight. Time one print with a stopwatch rather than trusting the estimate — the first print of an unfamiliar model always takes longer, and the tenth takes less. Then set the margin from your costs rather than from the competitor's price, and check what the competitor is actually selling: a $9.99 print that arrives without packaging or a tracked label is not the same product as yours. Finally, put the failure rate in even when the print feels reliable; it is the one input that turns a profitable month into a break-even one.
